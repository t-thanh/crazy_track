## Part A — is the deployment cell estimator-limited?

### Instrument

`--sensor-scale` multiplies `bias_std`, `jitter_std`, `vel_std` and `att_std_deg`
together, so one number moves overall measurement quality; `1.0` is the
literature-grounded model. **The gyro is deliberately left unscaled** — it is an onboard
IMU, not a Lighthouse product, and scaling it would have confounded the test with a
different sensor. The scale and the evaluation seed are now recorded in every run's
`metadata.yaml`.

80 rollouts: 4 scales × 4 stacks × 5 evaluation seeds, at the deployment cell
(normal tier, Lighthouse, constant wind). Split into two invocations per cell because the
hybrid stack runs in `rotor_vel` mode.

### Result

$\mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{3D}}$ (m), mean ± s.d. over 5 evaluation seeds:

| scale | Offset-free MPC | DATT-Asym | ADRC + xadapt | ADRC | spread | worst/best |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.25 | 0.048 ± 0.001 | 0.061 ± 0.001 | 0.050 ± 0.003 | 0.056 ± 0.003 | 0.014 | 1.28× |
| 0.50 | 0.049 ± 0.005 | 0.061 ± 0.004 | 0.052 ± 0.002 | 0.057 ± 0.002 | 0.012 | 1.24× |
| **1.00** | **0.057 ± 0.010** | **0.063 ± 0.010** | **0.054 ± 0.006** | **0.060 ± 0.005** | **0.009** | **1.17×** |
| 2.00 | 0.093 ± 0.045 | 0.077 ± 0.026 | 0.068 ± 0.020 | 0.073 ± 0.018 | 0.025 | 1.38× |

Figure: `publication1/figures/fig7_sensor_scale.pdf` (regenerate with
`python scripts/analyze_sensor_scale.py --figure`).

### What this says, against the pre-registered criteria

The request defined three outcomes. **The observed outcome is a combination of the second
and third, and it is not the first.**

**1. The claim as written is not supported.** "Estimator-limited" predicts that relaxing
the estimator relieves the binding constraint. Quartering every sensor error term buys:

| stack | 1.0 → 0.25 | improvement |
|---|---|---|
| Offset-free MPC | 0.057 → 0.048 | 16 % |
| ADRC | 0.060 → 0.056 | 7 % |
| ADRC + xadapt | 0.054 → 0.050 | 7 % |
| DATT-Asym | 0.063 → 0.061 | 3 % |

**A 4× better sensor buys between 3 % and 16 %.** That is not the signature of a binding
constraint; it is the signature of a constraint that has already stopped binding. Between
scale 0.5 and 0.25 — another halving — three of the four stacks move by
\SI{1}{\milli\meter} or less and DATT-Asym does not move at all. All four are flattening
onto a floor near \SI{0.05}{\meter} that better sensing does not lift.

**2. Sensing does bind in the other direction.** Doubling the error degrades every stack
(1.3–1.6×) and inflates the seed spread by a factor of three to five (offset-free MPC
±0.010 → ±0.045). So the cell is *sensor-sensitive*, but asymmetrically: worse sensing
hurts, better sensing does not help proportionally.

**3. The tie is real and is a property of this sensor quality.** The four stacks stay
within 9–25 mm of each other at every scale, and the tie is *tightest at exactly the
operating point the paper reports* (spread 0.009, ratio 1.17×), widening both as sensing
improves (1.28×) and as it degrades (1.38×). This is the request's second outcome: a more
precise and still publishable claim. The ordering also becomes stable as sensing improves
— offset-free MPC < ADRC+xadapt < ADRC < DATT-Asym at scale 0.25, with error bars an
order of magnitude tighter than at scale 1.0.

**4. One non-monotonicity.** DATT-Asym is flat within noise from 1.0 down to 0.25
(0.063 → 0.061 → 0.061), so it is formally non-monotone in the sweep. This is consistent
with Lesson 6: the policy was trained with sensor-noise domain randomization over
$\mathcal{U}(0,1.5)$, so scale 0.25 is inside its training range and it has learned to
average over it rather than exploit it. It is the stack that benefits least from a better
sensor, which is the predicted cost of randomization, now measured.

### What binds instead — not answered here

The floor is common to four unrelated architectures, so it is a property of the task, not
of any controller. Three candidates, in the order I would test them:

1. **The wind itself.** \SI{2.5}{\meter\per\second\squared} standing against
   \SI{8.6}{\meter\per\second\squared} of spare authority; every stack has finite
   disturbance-rejection bandwidth regardless of how well it senses.
2. **The actuator envelope / attitude-loop lag** at the normal tier.
3. **The reference**, whose curvature at $T = \SI{5.5}{\second}$ sets a tracking floor.

The discriminating run is one line — the same sweep with `--disturbance none` — and it
was **not** run, because the request scoped this round to the two listed experiments. If
the floor persists without wind, candidate 1 is out and the limit is the platform.

Existing data hints at candidate 1: with a clean sensor and the same wind, the pool
reaches 0.025–0.073 (Table 2's wind column), which straddles the floor rather than sitting
far below it. That is suggestive, not decisive, because the clean-sensor path has no
latency at all, so it is not the same limit taken to zero.

### Bottom line for the writing pass

The sentence *"at this sensor quality the deployment condition is estimator-limited, not
architecture-limited"* should not survive as written. What the data supports is narrower
and, I think, more interesting:

> Four unrelated architectures converge to within \SI{9}{\milli\meter} at this sensor
> quality, and the convergence is not relieved by better sensing — quartering the
> measurement error moves them by 3–16 % and they remain tied. The binding constraint at
> this operating point is therefore *shared* but is not the estimator; degrading the
> sensor separates them again, so sensing bounds the cell from above without setting its
> floor.

The "redirects engineering effort from controller design to state estimation" corollary in
the contributions bullet follows from the falsified reading and should go with it.

## Part B — the tie now compares like with like

10 evaluation seeds on the seed-0 training run: **$0.065\pm0.010$** (range 0.056–0.084),
against the $0.059\pm0.002$ over three training seeds that the paper reports.

All four members on the same footing:

| stack | evaluation seeds (n = 10) | paper currently |
|---|---|---|
| Offset-free MPC | $0.057\pm0.014$ | same |
| ADRC + xadapt | $0.060\pm0.009$ | same |
| ADRC | $0.063\pm0.006$ | same |
| DATT-Asym | $\mathbf{0.065\pm0.010}$ | $0.059\pm0.002$ (3 *training* seeds) |

**The tie survives and improves in rigour.** Best-to-worst is now \SI{8}{\milli\meter}
(was 6), still inside the widest member's spread, so every tie test the paper uses still
passes. The policy moves from best-of-the-tied to last-of-the-tied without leaving the
band. "Within \SI{6}{\milli\meter}" should become "within \SI{8}{\milli\meter}" in the
abstract, §4.3 and the conclusion.

**The measurement already existed.** `ms-lhwind-s*` (2026-07-22) is the same experiment,
and this run reproduces it bit-identically — same mean, same standard deviation, same
minimum and maximum. So the paper has been reporting the tighter of two available spreads
for the one member of the tie that was measured differently from the rest. The choice is
labelled in the caption and defensible on its own terms; it is worth recording that it is
also the choice that made that member look tightest.

**Consequence for Lesson 5.** Its "$\pm\SI{0.002}{\meter}$, the smallest spread we
measured anywhere, which is what makes it quotable" is true as a *training-seed*
statement — initialization barely matters for this cell — but it reads as a claim about
reproducibility in general, which the evaluation-seed spread (±0.010, comparable to the
other three) does not support. Two numbers, two meanings, as the request put it.

**Lesson 3 is unaffected.** Its variance-collapse claim ($0.126\pm0.007$ against
$0.323\pm0.106$) is already an evaluation-seed statement at the LH-fast cell, measured
from `ms-lh-s*`. Nothing there changes.

## Part C note — per-control-step cost (item 4)

Requested if cheap; it was. `scripts/bench_control_step.py` times `act()` alone — no
simulator, no logging — on an idle machine, 150 calls after warm-up:

| stack | median | p90 | share of the \SI{10}{\milli\second} period |
|---|---|---|---|
| ADRC | \SI{0.167}{\milli\second} | 0.231 | 1.7 % |
| PID + feedforward | \SI{0.172}{\milli\second} | 0.311 | 1.7 % |
| DATT-Asym | \SI{0.271}{\milli\second} | 0.397 | 2.7 % |
| ADRC + xadapt | \SI{0.337}{\milli\second} | 1.163 | 3.4 %$^{\dagger}$ |
| MPPI + $\mathcal{L}_1$ | \SI{5.380}{\milli\second} | 7.440 | 54 % |
| Plain MPC | \SI{30.5}{\milli\second} | 42.1 | **305 %** |
| Offset-free MPC | \SI{31.3}{\milli\second} | 39.1 | **313 %** |

$^{\dagger}$ its rate loop runs at \SI{500}{\hertz}, a \SI{2}{\milli\second} period, so
its real share is ~17 %.

This is a better number than the harness episode time in every way, and it changes what
the recommendation table says. **Both optimizers exceed the control period by roughly
3× in this implementation** — the stack the paper recommends for maximum precision is
not real-time feasible as built. MPPI fits with half the period to spare; everything
else is under 4 %.

Two caveats to keep with the number: this is Python/CasADi with ipopt on a laptop CPU,
not embedded C, and a compiled implementation would be substantially faster; and the
ratio (an optimizer costs ~180× the baseline per step) is the transferable quantity
rather than the absolute milliseconds.
