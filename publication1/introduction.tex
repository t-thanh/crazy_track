\section{INTRODUCTION}
\label{sec:intro}

Micro- and nano-scale unmanned aerial vehicles are attractive for inspection,
reconnaissance and confined-space operation precisely because they are small, and every
control difficulty they present follows from the same property. On a \SI{43}{\gram}
platform with a thrust-to-weight ratio below two, a \SI{10}{\gram} payload is a quarter
of the vehicle's weight, a light breeze is a substantial fraction of the available
lateral acceleration, and there is no margin to absorb either. Such vehicles rarely
carry laboratory-grade positioning: they fly on onboard estimates that update at a few
tens of hertz, carry centimeter-scale spatial bias, and lag the true state. Trajectory
tracking under wind, payload variation and degraded sensing is therefore not a
refinement of the nominal problem but a different problem, and it is the one that
determines whether a vehicle is useful outside a flight arena.

A large literature addresses it, from classical cascaded feedback through disturbance
observers, adaptive control and predictive control to end-to-end learned policies. What
that literature does not readily provide is a comparison. Results are reported on
different airframes, against different reference trajectories, with different error
metrics, and --- most consequentially --- under different assumptions about state
feedback, which alone can move tracking error by an order of magnitude. A further
asymmetry compounds the problem: papers report the conditions in which a method wins.
Failure modes, the information a practitioner most needs when selecting a controller,
are seldom characterized at all, and when they are reported it is usually as a mean over
a single run.

Two effects that this practice hides motivate the present study, and both recur
throughout it. The first is \emph{variance}. Under a randomly drawn but
episode-constant sensor bias, some controllers degrade uniformly while others are
usually excellent and occasionally close to failure; their means can be similar while
their deployment risk is not. The second is \emph{transients}. Benchmark references
conventionally begin at nonzero velocity while the vehicle starts at rest, and the fixed
warm-up window used to discount the resulting excursion does not always cover it ---
which invites attributing a launch artifact to steady-state noise sensitivity, as we
did ourselves before checking.

\subsection{Related work}

\paragraph{Tracking benchmarks.}
Eschmann et al.\cite{eschmann2024learning} established the figure-eight Lissajous
protocol we adopt, comparing PID, geometric, nonlinear and incremental controllers
against a learned policy at three cycle times with root-mean-square position error as
the metric. Huang et al.\cite{huang2023datt} evaluate adaptive learned tracking on both
smooth and dynamically infeasible references, in simulation and with a fan array. We
adopt the reference family and the metric of the former verbatim so that our numbers are
comparable in kind, and extend both with a shared disturbance and sensing matrix and
with per-cell seed statistics, neither of which the source protocols include.

\paragraph{Disturbance rejection and predictive tracking.}
Active disturbance rejection control\cite{han2009adrc} lumps unmodeled dynamics and
external forces into a single state estimated by an extended observer, tuned through a
bandwidth parameterization\cite{gao2003scaling}. $\mathcal{L}_1$ adaptive
control\cite{hovakimyan2010l1} separates fast estimation from filtered actuation to
retain robustness guarantees, and incremental nonlinear dynamic
inversion\cite{smeur2016indi} achieves related ends through measured accelerations. In
predictive control the analogous device is the offset-free
construction\cite{maeder2009offsetfree}, which augments the prediction model with a
disturbance state driven by an observer. Predictive tracking itself divides between
nonlinear MPC on identified closed-loop attitude models, the standard high-accuracy
option for small quadrotors, and path-integral sampling\cite{williams2018mppi}, which
tolerates non-smooth costs and models at the price of a sampling noise floor and a
dependence on exploration design; both inherit the validity limits of their internal
model. These mechanisms are usually compared theoretically or in isolation, and the
bandwidth-versus-noise trade-off they share is frequently asserted and rarely measured
across conditions on one platform.

\paragraph{Learned tracking.}
Policies that consume a preview window of the reference and an online disturbance
estimate\cite{huang2023datt} have matched or exceeded classical tracking in simulation
and in flight. Two training techniques recur in the recent literature: asymmetric
actor--critic schemes\cite{pinto2018asymmetric}, in which a privileged critic sees the
true state while the actor sees only deployable observations, and domain randomization
over sensor noise. Separately, learned low-level controllers trained across many
airframes\cite{zhang2025xadapt} adapt online to vehicles they have never seen, which
makes them composable with classical outer loops. Across all three threads the open
question is the same and is symmetric: published comparisons rarely tune every candidate
\emph{under the same conditions} --- most tune the method under study carefully and its
alternatives casually --- so the reported differences confound paradigm with tuning
effort. That is the gap this benchmark is built to close.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{itemize}
\item A \textbf{cross-paradigm benchmark}: six trajectory-tracking controllers, one per
      paradigm and each the best member of its own family in our data, evaluated on a
      single nano-quadrotor under a shared matrix of constant wind, gusts, ground
      effect, a \SI{23}{\percent} payload step and a literature-grounded optical
      positioning model, with ten evaluation seeds and three training seeds so that
      every cell carries an error bar.
\item The \textbf{deployment-cell convergence result}: under degraded onboard sensing
      with wind, an offset-free MPC, a privileged-critic reinforcement-learning policy,
      an extended state observer over a learned adaptive rate loop, and that same
      observer driving the firmware attitude loop all become statistically
      indistinguishable at approximately \SI{0.06}{\meter}. We argue that
      this regime is estimator-limited rather than architecture-limited, which redirects
      engineering effort from controller design to state estimation.
\item A \textbf{lessons-learnt analysis} organised around a three-class failure taxonomy,
      including three occasions on which the protocol overturned our own earlier reading
      of the data --- a launch transient misdiagnosed as noise fragility, a single
      evaluation seed that proved to be the minimum of ten draws, and a variance figure
      whose three-seed estimate overstated both mean and spread --- and two documented
      negative results reported with their mechanisms.
\item A \textbf{hardware validation protocol} in a programmable fan-array wind tunnel
      with motion-capture ground truth, constructed so that the simulated disturbance
      classes map one-to-one onto physical set points, with three falsifiable targets
      stated in advance.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes
the platform, the benchmark, the six controllers, the disturbance and sensing models,
the hardware validation setup and the statistical protocol.
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports nominal, disturbance and realistic-sensing results,
followed by the lessons and condition-indexed recommendations.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.
