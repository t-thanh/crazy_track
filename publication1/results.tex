\section{RESULTS AND LESSONS LEARNT}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Nominal tracking}
\label{sec:results:nominal}

Table~\ref{tab:nominal} reports tracking accuracy with clean state feedback and no
external disturbance. Before interpreting it, one qualitative check: the spatial error
signature reproduces the one reported for this benchmark in the
literature\cite{eschmann2024learning} --- near-exact tracking at the slow tier, mild
corner cutting at normal, and systematic outward overshoot at the lobes at fast, where
centripetal acceleration peaks (Fig.~\ref{fig:tracking}). The benchmark therefore
behaves like the published one before we perturb it.

Three observations set up everything that follows. First, the predictive controller with
a disturbance state sets records at both ends of the speed range, but it is not alone at
the millimetre level: at the slow tier the extended state observer reaches
\SI{0.005}{\meter} and the hybrid adaptive stack \SI{0.010}{\meter}, so three unrelated
stacks are all within a centimetre of a reference they have fifteen seconds to follow.
The ordering at the fast tier is the informative one, and there the optimizer is alone
(\SI{0.052}{\meter} against \SI{0.098}{\meter} for the observer and \SI{0.124}{\meter}
for the hybrid stack) --- with clean state feedback and a demanding reference, an
accurate optimizer is very hard to beat. Second, the reinforcement-learning policy is the \emph{worst}
stack in this table by a wide margin, roughly three times the baseline at the normal
tier. That is not a training failure; it is the price of the domain randomization that
buys its later results, and quantifying that price is Lesson~6. Third, the spread
between the three best stacks at any single speed is smaller than the spread induced by
changing the speed, which is a warning that nominal tables discriminate poorly between
competent controllers.

The mechanisms behind each column are distinct, and the winners follow the mechanisms
rather than the paradigms (Fig.~\ref{fig:disturbance}). Under \emph{constant wind}, the baseline degrades fivefold,
from $0.022$ to \SI{0.109}{\meter}, because its integrator saturates at
\SI{1}{\meter\per\second\squared} and simply cannot lift a
\SI{2.5}{\meter\per\second\squared} standing load; the extended state observer, which
has no such limit, holds \SI{0.025}{\meter} --- essentially its undisturbed value. Under
\emph{gusts} the ranking changes: what matters is no longer whether a disturbance can be
cancelled but how fast the estimate tracks, and the learned policies, which receive an
$\mathcal{L}_1$ estimate directly in their observation and can act on it without waiting
for position error to accumulate, take the column. Under \emph{payload} the hybrid
adaptive stack is the outlier: at \SI{0.019}{\meter} it is within a fifth of its own
undisturbed value, so a \SI{23}{\percent} mass increase costs it almost nothing, against
a factor of four for the baseline --- Lesson~7. \emph{Ground effect} is the mildest
column in the table: the multiplicative thrust gain is nearly collinear with the
feedforward the baseline already applies, so the baseline holds \SI{0.023}{\meter}
without any disturbance estimate at all, and the observer-based stacks --- once their
bandwidth is set for noise rather than for gusts --- match or beat it rather than
spending bandwidth chasing it (\SI{0.016}{\meter} for the hybrid adaptive stack,
\SI{0.023}{\meter} for the optimizer, \SI{0.024}{\meter} for the observer). Only the
learned policy, which never saw a thrust-gain disturbance in training, is clearly worse
at \SI{0.089}{\meter}.

The organising claim of this table is not who wins but why anyone does: \textbf{every
stack that beats the baseline under a sustained disturbance does so through some form of
lumped-disturbance estimation.} The paradigms differ in where the estimate is injected
--- into the control law, into the prediction model, into the policy's observation, or
into a low-level adaptation loop --- not in whether one is required. The plain MPC
context row makes the point negatively: it is the most sophisticated controller in the
table by construction, and, lacking any disturbance state, it is also the worst under
every force disturbance.

\subsection{Realistic sensing and the deployment condition}
\label{sec:results:deployment}

Table~\ref{tab:sensing} reports the conditions that matter for a
vehicle that must fly on its own sensors. Under the Lighthouse model alone, absolute
errors rise for every stack, and the ordering shifts: the observer-based controllers,
which differentiate a \SI{34}{\hertz} zero-order-hold position signal, give up much of
their advantage, while the policy trained with that noise in the loop barely moves.
Adding wind on top produces the deployment condition and the central result of this
paper, shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:deployment}:

\noindent Four architectures with nothing in common --- an optimizer carrying a
disturbance state, a neural policy trained with a privileged critic, a classical observer
stacked on a learned rate controller, and that same observer driving the firmware
attitude loop directly --- arrive within \SI{6}{\milli\meter} of each other, inside their
own seed spreads. The convergence is itself the finding. Each of these stacks sits
considerably further from its own clean-sensing performance than from the other three:
the optimizer gives up an order of magnitude relative to its \SI{0.036}{\meter} nominal,
the hybrid stack quadruples its \SI{0.015}{\meter}, the observer nearly triples its
\SI{0.023}{\meter}, and the policy is the only one that barely moves --- and it started
worst. When four unrelated mechanisms saturate at the same value, the binding constraint
is not any of them. \textbf{At this sensor quality the deployment condition is
estimator-limited, not architecture-limited}, and effort spent on a better controller is
effort misspent relative to effort spent on state estimation. The stacks that do
\emph{not} reach the band are equally instructive: the two without any disturbance
estimate sit at $0.116$ and \SI{0.199}{\meter}, so the price of entry (Lesson~1) is paid
under realistic sensing exactly as it is under clean sensing.

One caveat is stated rather than buried: the tied value is specific to this sensing
quality; with motion-capture-grade state the same four stacks are clearly separated
(Table~\ref{tab:nominal}). A second caveat has since been resolved, and how it resolved
is worth recording. In an earlier draft of this table the plain observer appeared at
\SI{0.053}{\meter} --- nominally better than every tied stack --- from a single
evaluation seed, and we declined to read it on the authority of our own Lesson~3. The
ten-seed re-run puts it at $0.063\pm0.006$~\si{\meter}, and \SI{0.053}{\meter} turns out
to be the \emph{minimum} of the ten draws. The single seed was not merely noisy; it was
the most favourable sample available, and reading it would have produced a false claim of
a new best stack. The correction adds a fourth member to the tie rather than a winner.

\subsection{Lessons learnt}
\label{sec:results:lessons}

The value of a benchmark of this kind lies less in the ranking than in what the failures
teach. Every failure we observed falls into one of three classes --- missing model
structure, estimator bandwidth-versus-noise trade-offs, and information bottlenecks in
learned policies --- and the seven lessons below are organised around them. Two of these
lessons correct conclusions we had ourselves drawn and reported from earlier, weaker
evidence.

\paragraph{Lesson 1: disturbance estimation is the price of entry.}
No controller in the pool beats the feedforward baseline under a sustained disturbance
without estimating that disturbance in some form. The corollary is that the paradigm
label is nearly irrelevant to robustness: what predicts performance is whether a lumped
disturbance is estimated, at what bandwidth, and where the estimate enters the loop.
This reframes a comparison usually posed as \emph{classical versus learned} into one
about estimator placement.

\paragraph{Lesson 2: one observer bandwidth cannot serve two purposes.}
Sweeping the extended-state-observer bandwidth over
$\omega_o \in \{3,5,7,10\}$~\si{\radian\per\second} across six conditions produced a
clean crossing rather than an optimum: low bandwidth wins under sensor noise and
constant loads (\SI{0.052}{\meter} at $\omega_o=3$ under Lighthouse, against
\SI{0.096}{\meter} at $\omega_o=10$), high bandwidth wins under \SI{0.7}{\hertz} gusts
(\SI{0.080}{\meter} against \SI{0.110}{\meter}), and no fixed value wins both. The
obvious remedy fails for an instructive reason. An innovation-scheduled adaptive
bandwidth --- raising $\omega_o$ when the observer innovation is biased rather than
white --- did win its target gust cells (\SI{0.071}{\meter} against \SI{0.080}{\meter}
for the best fixed value) while degrading every calm cell (\SI{0.060}{\meter} against
\SI{0.023}{\meter}). The scheduling statistic cannot distinguish an external disturbance
from the observer's \emph{own} attitude-loop lag residual during aggressive tracking, so
it false-positives into high bandwidth exactly when it should not. This is a property of
fixed-structure lumped-disturbance observers, not of our implementation, and we report
it as a negative result with its mechanism.

\paragraph{Lesson 3: mean-only tables mislead, sometimes decisively.}
Two stacks that look competitive on their means are not. The sampling-based controller
owns the fast tier with clean sensing at \SI{0.068}{\meter}, but under the Lighthouse
model the same cell measures $0.228\pm0.178$~\si{\meter} across seeds, ranging from
$0.091$ to \SI{0.480}{\meter}: on an unlucky bias draw, the temporally correlated
exploration that helps it nominally steers the whole sample distribution wrong for many
consecutive steps. The clean-trained policy shows the same shape,
$0.323\pm0.106$~\si{\meter}, some seeds fine and some near-crashing. Against this, the
benchmark policy's advantage is best described not as a lower mean but as a
\emph{variance collapse}: $0.126\pm0.007$~\si{\meter} in the same cell. A table of means
would have shown a difference; it would not have shown that one controller occasionally
fails and the other never does, which is the difference that matters for deployment
(Fig.~\ref{fig:variance}).

\paragraph{Lesson 4: audit transients before blaming steady-state noise.}
The offset-free optimizer initially measured $0.178\pm0.076$~\si{\meter} under the
Lighthouse model, and the natural reading --- that its observer was ingesting
zero-order-hold position noise --- is what we first reported. A bandwidth sweep appeared
to confirm it. Inspecting the error time series falsified it: the entire excess was a
single $0.7$--\SI{1.5}{\meter} excursion between $t = 0.5$ and \SI{1.6}{\second}, whose
tail extends past the \SI{1}{\second} warm-up exclusion, and steady-state error beyond
\SI{2.5}{\second} was $0.046\pm0.008$~\si{\meter} --- on par with the best stacks in
that cell (Fig.~\ref{fig:transient}). The cause was a cold observer whose first

innovations are noise-dominated at full gain, against a reference that begins at
\SI{1.14}{\meter\per\second} while the vehicle is at rest; planning against that phantom
disturbance during launch amplified the excursion. Ramping the estimate in over
\SI{1.5}{\second}, Eq.~\eqref{eq:softstart}, reduced the cell to
$0.141\pm0.058$~\si{\meter} and, unexpectedly, set new records in every nominal cell
(Table~\ref{tab:nominal}) --- the cold-start jitter had been costing performance even
with perfect sensing. A discriminating test then showed the residual is not the
observer's fault at all: on the same bad seeds, plain MPC without any disturbance state
spikes identically, to $0.215$ and \SI{0.230}{\meter}. The transient is generic to
optimizing controllers meeting a reference-velocity step through a noisy state estimate.
The transferable procedure is simple: \emph{check when the maximum error occurs before
attributing an error level to steady-state noise}, and report whether a warm-up window
actually covers the transient it was chosen to remove.

\paragraph{Lesson 5: single-seed conclusions about learned policies invert.}
From one training seed per configuration we concluded --- and wrote --- that the
asymmetric-critic recipe improved the noisy cells at the cost of clean-state
performance. At three training seeds the comparison reverses: the earlier policy had
drawn its \emph{best} nominal initialization and the newer one its \emph{worst}, and on
the mean the newer policy dominates or ties the older in all eight measured cells. The
retracted claim was not marginal; it was a stated design trade-off that did not exist.
Meanwhile the deployment-cell result survived the same test with the smallest spread we
measured anywhere ($\pm\SI{0.002}{\meter}$), which is what makes it quotable. Training
variance in this setting is comparable to the effect sizes being claimed --- in the
slow-tier nominal cell it is $\pm\SI{0.015}{\meter}$ against effects of a similar size
--- so single-seed policy comparisons in that regime carry no information at all.

\paragraph{Lesson 6: robustness is bought with agility at a measurable exchange rate.}
The three policy variants form a clean ladder. Training with an $\mathcal{L}_1$ channel
and force randomization gives the best clean-sensing tracking and the best gust
rejection, and fails under noisy sensing at high agility (\SI{0.323}{\meter}). Adding
the sensor model to training fixes that failure ($\rightarrow\SI{0.126}{\meter}$) and
costs both clean-state accuracy and disturbance rejection, because noise in the
disturbance channel during training teaches the policy to discount that channel ---
robustness purchased by lowering effective feedback gain. Adding a privileged critic and
noise-level randomization recovers part of the disturbance rejection and produces the
deployment result, but does not recover clean-state agility, and the reason is
structural: a memoryless network cannot infer the current episode's noise level from a
single observation frame, so it averages over the randomization range instead of
calibrating to the episode. Testing that diagnosis by stacking four observation frames
did not help at this training budget --- an \SI{80}{\milli\second} window spans only two
or three position updates, which is thin evidence for inferring a noise level, and the
fourfold larger input came with no additional capacity. Recurrence, or explicit
innovation-variance features, is the indicated next step. The general point:
\emph{domain randomization does not make a policy adaptive; it makes it average}, unless
the architecture can observe the thing being randomized.

\paragraph{Lesson 7: mechanisms compose when they address disjoint error sources.}
Stacking an extended state observer on a learned low-level rate controller produced the
broadest stack in the pool --- it wins four of the five columns of
Table~\ref{tab:matrix} --- and payload tracking within a fifth of its own undisturbed
value ($0.019$ against \SI{0.015}{\meter}), where the baseline degrades by a factor of
four. Neither layer achieves this alone: the observer cancels external \emph{forces} in
the acceleration command, while the learned layer makes the \emph{realization} of a rate
command airframe-independent underneath it. The composition also has two measurable costs
that follow from the same structure. Under constant wind the stack loses to the plain
observer ($0.040$ against \SI{0.025}{\meter}), because the observer now watches the plant
through an adaptive inner loop that partially absorbs --- and therefore masks --- the very
signal it is estimating. And at the fast tier it is the weakest of the classical stacks
(\SI{0.124}{\meter} against \SI{0.052}{\meter} for the optimizer), because its outer loop
runs decimated relative to the \SI{500}{\hertz} inner loop and that lag is exposed exactly
where the reference is most demanding. Layered adaptation is not free; it is a trade of
estimator observability and outer-loop bandwidth for actuation invariance.

\subsection{Recommendations}
\label{sec:results:recommendations}

No controller in this pool dominates, so a recommendation must be indexed by the
condition (Table~\ref{tab:recommend}). The most consequential entry is the last: once
sensing is realistic, the choice among the four tied stacks should be made on
engineering cost rather than accuracy, and their costs differ by orders of magnitude ---
the optimizer requires tens of seconds of solver time per episode in our implementation,
while the observer and the policy cost a handful of arithmetic operations and a single
forward pass through a two-layer network respectively (Table~\ref{tab:recommend}).
